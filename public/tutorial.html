Visual TOPSIS-RAD

An interactive app for ranking alternatives with TOPSIS-RAD — Ranking According to Desires. Instead of anchoring the ideal solutions to the best and worst values in the data, TOPSIS-RAD anchors them to the decision maker’s desired and vetoed performance levels.

Stable, desire-driven rankings — robust to outliers and to rank reversal.

1. Overview

Visual TOPSIS-RAD is a web application for multicriteria ranking. It implements both classic TOPSIS (Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution) and the proposed TOPSIS-RAD method, which grounds the ranking in the decision maker’s explicit aspirations rather than in the extremes of the observed data.

Why TOPSIS-RAD? Classic TOPSIS derives its reference points — the Positive Ideal Solution (PIS) and Negative Ideal Solution (NIS) — from the observed alternatives. This makes rankings vulnerable to three problems: (1) misalignment with what the decision maker actually wants, (2) sensitivity to outliers, and (3) rank reversal when alternatives are added or removed. TOPSIS-RAD addresses all three by using two arrays of decision-maker-defined reference levels.

What does the app do?

  • Accepts a performance matrix (alternatives × criteria) via CSV upload or a built-in template (including the TOPSIS-RAD paper example).
  • Lets you set criteria weights with an interactive radar or sliders (with per-criterion locks).
  • For TOPSIS-RAD, lets you enter Vetoed (VPL) and Desired (DPL) performance levels per criterion.
  • Screens out non-viable alternatives (VPL), caps performances at the desired level (DPL), and normalizes against these fixed frontiers.
  • Produces a ranking, charts (bar, radar, decision-matrix heatmap), the full chain of intermediate matrices, and a downloadable CSV.
  • Includes a rank-reversal simulator and an animated didactic mode.

2. Quick Start

  1. Open the app — visit topsis-ranking.vercel.app. You land on the Data tab.
  2. Load data — in the “Template” dropdown pick “TOPSIS-RAD paper example” and click Load template, or upload your own CSV.
  3. Choose the method — under Method, select TOPSIS-RAD (or TOPSIS for the classic method).
  4. Set the weights — distribute criteria importance with the radar or the sliders.
  5. Set DPL & VPL — in TOPSIS-RAD settings (DPL/VPL), enter the desired and minimum-acceptable level for each criterion.
  6. Calculate — click Calculate ranking; the app jumps to the Ranking tab.
  7. Explore & export — browse the Charts and Matrices tabs, then click Export CSV.
Tip: Start with the built-in TOPSIS-RAD paper example and the default DPL/VPL values to get a feel for the interface before entering your own reference levels.

Editing and criterion direction

After loading, the Dataset editor shows an editable table. You can change names and values, add or remove rows, set each criterion’s direction (Benefit = higher is better, or Cost = lower is better), and Save as CSV. Two stat cards report the number of alternatives and criteria.

Note: Direction matters for TOPSIS-RAD. For a benefit criterion, VPL is a minimum acceptable value and DPL a desired ceiling; for a cost criterion the roles flip (VPL is the maximum acceptable, DPL the desired floor). The app handles both automatically.

Under the Method control you choose between:

5. Criteria Weights

Weights express the relative importance of the criteria. Both methods use them identically. Under Distribute weights you have two equivalent editors:

Radar

Drag the sliders around a radar plot. The total is always 100 (shown normalized to 1). Great for seeing the balance of importance at a glance.

Sliders

A vertical list of sliders (0–100), one per criterion, with the normalized value beside each.

Each criterion has a lock toggle: lock a weight to hold it fixed while the others redistribute. The sum is renormalized to 1 automatically before the calculation.

Tip: Use Redistribute weights to reset to an equal split across all criteria.

6. Reference Levels: DPL & VPL TOPSIS-RAD

This is the heart of TOPSIS-RAD. In the TOPSIS-RAD settings (DPL/VPL) panel you set, for each criterion, two decision-maker-defined boundaries:

VPL — Vetoed Performance Level

The minimum acceptable performance (for a benefit criterion). Any alternative that fails to reach the VPL on any criterion is vetoed — removed from the feasible set before the ranking is computed.

For a cost criterion, VPL is the maximum acceptable value.

DPL — Desired Performance Level

The desired performance (for a benefit criterion). Performances above the DPL are capped to it: exceeding the desire brings no extra reward, so a single spectacular value can no longer dominate the ranking.

For a cost criterion, DPL is the desired floor.

Two levels, two names. The values you type — DPL and VPL — are raw performance levels, used before normalization to cap and to veto. After normalization they become the DNL (Desired Normalized Level) and VNL (Vetoed Normalized Level), which take the place of PIS and NIS. See Section 10.

Defaults. When you switch to TOPSIS-RAD, the app pre-fills VPL with each criterion’s column minimum and DPL with its column maximum (reversed for cost criteria). With these defaults, nothing is vetoed and nothing is capped, so TOPSIS-RAD reproduces a Min–Max TOPSIS. Change the values to encode your actual desires.

Important: Set VPL below the values you consider unacceptable (too high and you may veto everything), and DPL at the level beyond which you are indifferent (too low and you flatten real distinctions). The walkthrough shows sensible choices.

7. Results

7.1 Ranking tab

Four stat cards summarize the run: Alternatives, Criteria, 1st place, and Max score. Below, the Final ranking table lists every alternative with its position and closeness score Siw ∈ [0, 1] (higher = better). Click Export CSV to save it.

In TOPSIS-RAD, vetoed alternatives are excluded from the table rather than ranked last.

7.2 Charts tab

  • Ranking by score — a bar chart of the closeness scores, with dashed reference lines for the best (upper) and worst (lower) values. Each alternative keeps a consistent identity color shared with the radar.
  • Criteria comparison (matrix T) — a radar of the weighted normalized matrix for the top alternatives, with the reference frontiers (PIS/NIS — DNL/VNL in RAD) drawn as dashed guides.
  • Decision matrix (G) — Heatmap — the raw performance matrix shaded by value, with per-column max and min rows.

7.3 Matrices tab

The full, expandable chain of intermediate matrices — from the decision matrix G, through the normalized R and weighted T, to the reference vectors, distances, and scores — so every number in the ranking is traceable.

8. Rank-Reversal Analysis

On the Rank Reversal tab you can simulate removing alternatives and see how the ranking of the remaining ones changes. Rank reversal happens when the relative order of two alternatives flips just because the set of alternatives changed — a classic weakness of data-anchored methods.

  1. Select one or more alternatives to remove.
  2. Analyze rank reversal — the app recomputes and shows a comparison of original vs. new ranks and scores, flagging any reversals.
Why TOPSIS-RAD helps: because the DNL/VNL frontiers come from your fixed DPL/VPL — not from the data — they do not move when alternatives are removed. In this simulation those boundaries stay locked, so surviving alternatives keep their scores and reversal is structurally suppressed.

9. Didactic Mode

The Didactic Mode tab plays an animated, step-by-step calculation: normalized weights, the normalized and weighted matrices, the reference vectors, the distances dib (to DNL/PIS) and diw (to VNL/NIS), and the final scores. Use Play/Pause, Previous/Next, or jump to a specific step. It is the fastest way to see how the ranking is built.

10. Methodological Background

10.1 The TOPSIS-RAD routine

Given alternatives A, criteria C, decision matrix G, weights w, directions p, and the reference arrays DPL = {dj} and VPL = {vj}, the routine is:

  1. Inputs — collect A, C, G, w, p and the DPL/VPL arrays.
  2. Normalize the weights so that ∑wj = 1.
  3. Qualify — keep only alternatives that meet the veto level on every criterion (benefit: gij ≥ vj; cost: gij ≤ vj). The rest are vetoed.
  4. Cap at DPL — build the desired-constrained matrix Gd:
    gdij = min(gij, dj)  (benefit)   ·   max(gij, dj)  (cost)
  5. Normalize against the frontiers — not Min–Max over the data, but against the VPL (lower) and DPL (upper) bounds:
    rij = ( gdij − vj ) / ( dj − vj )
    This is what makes the frontiers independent of the alternative set. Values are clamped to [0, 1].
  6. Weight — tij = wj · rij.
  7. Reference levels — because normalization maps DPL → 1 and VPL → 0, the weighted frontiers are known in closed form (benefit shown):
    DNLj = wj   ·   VNLj = 0
    These replace PIS and NIS — and they never depend on the data.
  8. Distances — Euclidean distance of each alternative to the frontiers: dib to DNL and diw to VNL.
  9. Closeness —
    Siw = diw / ( dib + diw )
  10. Rank the surviving alternatives by decreasing Siw.

10.2 DPL/VPL vs. DNL/VNL

Keep the two levels straight:
  • DPL / VPL — the raw performance levels you type in. Used before normalization, to cap (DPL) and to veto (VPL).
  • DNL / VNL — the normalized levels derived afterward. They substitute for PIS / NIS in the distance calculation.

10.3 How this differs from classic TOPSIS

AspectClassic TOPSISTOPSIS-RAD

Classic TOPSIS in this app follows Hwang & Yoon (1981). An Advanced (algorithm) panel lets the classic method use Min–Max or vector normalization; TOPSIS-RAD always uses its own frontier-based normalization.

11. Frequently Asked Questions

When should I use TOPSIS-RAD instead of classic TOPSIS?

Whenever you can articulate what “good enough” (DPL) and “unacceptable” (VPL) mean for each criterion, or when your data has outliers, or when you need rankings that stay stable as alternatives are added or removed.

What happens to a vetoed alternative?

It is removed from the feasible set before ranking — it simply does not appear in the results. An alternative is vetoed if it fails the VPL on any single criterion.

What if I don’t know good DPL/VPL values yet?

Leave the defaults (column max/min). With them, nothing is capped or vetoed and TOPSIS-RAD matches Min–Max TOPSIS. Then adjust one criterion at a time and watch the ranking respond.

Can I mix benefit and cost criteria?

Yes. Set each criterion’s direction in the Dataset editor. For cost criteria the VPL/DPL roles flip automatically (VPL = maximum acceptable, DPL = desired floor).

Why does an outstanding value stop helping past a point?

Because DPL caps it: once a performance reaches the desired level, exceeding it adds no further credit. This is deliberate — it prevents a single outlier from distorting the ranking.

How do I reproduce the paper’s example?

Load the TOPSIS-RAD paper example template and follow Appendix A, which lists the exact weights, VPL, and DPL used and the resulting ranking.

Appendix A: Step-by-Step Walkthrough

Ranking the built-in TOPSIS-RAD paper example, and seeing the desires at work

This walkthrough uses the built-in TOPSIS-RAD paper example — 10 alternatives (A1–A10) evaluated on 4 benefit criteria (C1–C4). Notice two outliers (A3 scores 150 on C3; A5 scores 118 on C4) and one clearly weak alternative (A8 = 15, 20, 5, 41). We rank with equal weights and the following decision-maker levels:

C1C2C3C4
VPL (min acceptable)50504050
DPL (desired)9088100100

1 Load the paper example

On the Data tab, choose “TOPSIS-RAD paper example” in the Template dropdown and click Load template. The Dataset editor fills with 10 alternatives × 4 criteria; the stat cards read Alternatives: 10 and Criteria: 4. All four criteria default to Benefit.

2 Select TOPSIS-RAD

Under Method, click TOPSIS-RAD. The TOPSIS-RAD settings (DPL/VPL) panel appears with one row per criterion, pre-filled with the column min (VPL) and max (DPL).

3 Set equal weights

Leave the weights equal (25 each → 0.25 normalized), or click Redistribute weights to enforce an even split. This isolates the effect of the reference levels.

4 Enter the desired and vetoed levels

In the DPL/VPL panel, type the values from the table above. Two things are now encoded:

  • VPL declares the minimum acceptable performance. A8 (15, 20, 5, 41) fails every criterion — it will be vetoed.
  • DPL declares the desired level. The outliers exceed it and will be capped: A3’s C3 150→100, A5’s C4 118→100, and A7’s C4 105→100.

5 Calculate the ranking

Click Calculate ranking. The app switches to the Ranking tab. A8 does not appear — it was vetoed. The nine survivors are ranked:

Three effects stand out, all driven by the reference levels:

  • A7 → A4 at the top. Part of A7’s classic edge was its C4 = 105. Once DPL caps C4 at 100, that edge shrinks and A4 takes first place.
  • A3 falls from 4th to 6th. A3’s classic strength was the C3 = 150 spike; capped to 100, its outlier advantage disappears.
  • A8 is vetoed, not ranked last. Rather than occupying the bottom with score 0, the non-viable option is removed from consideration entirely.

7 Read the charts and matrices

On the Charts tab, the bar chart shows the nine scores and the radar compares the top alternatives against the DNL/VNL frontiers. On the Matrices tab, expand the steps to see the capped matrix (A3’s C3 now reads 100), the frontier-normalized R, the weighted T, the DNL/VNL vectors, and the distances that produce each score.

8 Test rank-reversal robustness

On the Rank Reversal tab, remove, say, A5 and re-analyze. Because the DNL/VNL frontiers are fixed by your DPL/VPL — not by the surviving data — the remaining alternatives keep their scores and their order. This is the stability TOPSIS-RAD is designed for.

Summary of the Walkthrough

Using equal weights on the paper example, with VPL = (50, 50, 40, 50) and DPL = (90, 88, 100, 100):

  1. The VPL vetoes the non-viable A8, so it never competes.
  2. The DPL caps the outliers (A3’s C3, A5’s and A7’s C4), so no single spectacular value dominates.
  3. The ranking reflects balanced achievement of the desired levels — A4 leads — rather than raw extremes, and it is stable when the alternative set changes.
